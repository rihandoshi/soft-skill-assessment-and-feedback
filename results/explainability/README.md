# Explainability results (audio branch)

One folder per stage of `markdown/EXPLAINABILITY_STAGES.md`. Inside every stage folder the same layout:

- `report.md` (or `*_report.md`): generated by the stage script; re-running the script overwrites it.
- `notes.md`: hand-written interpretation; never overwritten.
- `tables/`: CSV outputs. `plots/`: figures. Stage-specific extras are listed below.

All paths are defined once in `scripts/explainability/paths.py`.

## Results so far

1. Answer **length** is the one delivery behaviour RecruitView raters penalise within the same person; loudness and absolute pitch only identify the speaker or microphone (Stage 0, 3).
2. Every answer has 50+ descriptors; extreme values were checked against Whisper and a second pitch tracker, and seven extraction bugs were fixed (Stage 1).
3. Reference tables are fitted on train participants only and transfer to validation. Every answer gets a hard class in each of the seven audio categories (e.g. Pace: slow / typical / fast; Expressiveness: monotone / typical / highly varied); Pace and Conciseness are used for feedback, the other five are experimental until validated. Active flags: `too_fast`, `long_answer` (Stage 2).
4. The interpretable signals reach ~90% of WavLM's accuracy (~97% with length), but within a person almost nothing besides length holds, because RecruitView's per-answer score differences are mostly rating noise (Stage 3).
5. Verbatim ASR replaced the cleaned transcripts: real sentences instead of subtitle lines, real word counts. Trailing off, measured at real sentence ends, now goes with lower scores within a person (weak); `too_slow` is back as experimental; fillers are handed to the text branch (Stage 4).
6. A pretrained speech-emotion model mostly measures the speaker (87%), cannot separate dominance from arousal (ρ 0.98), and raters score *calmer* speakers as more confident (ρ −0.50 between people). The composure class now describes activation relative to the speaker's usual (Stage 5). A second, independently trained model (WavLM) agrees (within-speaker ρ 0.73–0.75) and reproduces these findings.
7. Loudness is mostly a recording artefact: its relation to the scores disappears once recording quality is controlled. Answers in the worst 10% of audio quality keep pace / pausing / conciseness feedback but lose the pitch, energy and emotion-based categories (Stage 6).
8. On MIT (independent raters), Expressiveness validates clearly (pitch variability vs Engaging tone +0.32) and Conciseness holds; Pausing is strong but halo; Composure is not supported (MIT raters reward activation, RecruitView raters calm); `too_fast` is contradicted; Whisper misses ~93% of fillers (Stage 7).
9. Per-answer detectors (Stage 8, closed in Phase 1):
   - Fluency is audio only. It combines two pretrained disfluency classifiers with mid-sentence pauses: −0.63 with MIT `NoFillers`, −0.58 after controlling for length and quality.
   - Expressiveness is the emotion model's vocal activation, adjusted for voice pitch: +0.63 with `EngagingTone` after the controls. A second emotion model confirms it.
   - The confidence model is contradicted on MIT and not used.
   - Answer length is left to the text branch.
10. Evidence JSON v2 (Stage 9) carries only validated, audio-only observations, each with a time span.
11. Live interviews need their own reference (Stage 10). Every component shifts; against recorded clips 45% of live answers were "hesitated a lot". With one reference per setting, `low` and `high` are 18–21% for every dimension in both settings, and the MIT relations are unchanged (fluency −0.58, expressiveness +0.65 after controls).
12. No audio model of vocal confidence holds up (Stage 11). The speaking-style tagger's confidence value has no relation to MIT ratings. Every confidence model retrained on RecruitView, even with length and quality removed, is *contradicted* on MIT (partial −0.34 to −0.48 with `Overall`). The reason: RecruitView's raters heard calm, low-energy, lower-pitched delivery as confident, and MIT's raters rate that delivery lower. Clarity is dropped too: the tagger calls Indian-English answers "slurred" (median 0.92 against 0.06 on MIT), which is accent, not clarity. The audio branch's validated skills are fluency and expressiveness.
13. A new recording can be analysed without any dataset table (Stage 12). One answer through `analyze_answer.py` reproduces its dataset row to rounding error (30 validation answers; all levels equal except 3 at a cut). One MIT interview runs end to end with one command (41 s, 1.9 GB GPU). Cutting the answers out of an interview without a transcript **fails** its test: with an ungated speaker model, 75% of held-out answers are found and 59% within 3 s (bars 90% and 85%). With the Turker speaker labels, the same rules would pass (94% and 88%), so a proper diarization model (pyannote, needs a Hugging Face token) is the missing piece. Segmentation is deferred (decision of 16 Nov).
14. The audio outputs are ready for late fusion (Stage 13). The 12 audio score predictions (out-of-fold on train; val Spearman 0.24–0.43, test 0.25–0.55) are one signal (first component 94%) that follows speaking time (ρ ≈ −0.6). The feedback dimensions add to it: hesitation is nearly independent of it (0.02) and expressiveness runs against it (−0.39). Next: fusion (teammate), then one demo of the full pipeline.
15. The interview-level layer is built (Stage 14, pre-registered). Response latency (interviewer's last word to the first answer word) relates to `Overall` (−0.38) and `NotAwkward` (−0.27) after length and quality, and still after hesitation, so the summary can say "often took a while before starting to answer". Trends mostly do not hold up: within an interview, hesitation changes no more than random reordering of the answers would produce, so no hesitation trend is stated; expressiveness changes slightly more than chance (11 of 133 interviews) but the change adds nothing to the ratings beyond the average, so it is description only. An audio-only Ridge over the interview aggregates reaches 0.60 with `Overall` (out-of-fold by student), a baseline for fusion.

`stage3_validate_recruitview.py` (~12 min) → `fit_reference_distributions.py` again (it reads Stage 3's decisions).

Stages 8–9 (after both chains): `run_crisper_asr.py` (GPU, text branch hand-over) → `run_disfluency_classifier.py` (GPU) → `run_speech_flow.py` (~27 min on a 6-GB laptop GPU, cached) → `run_confidence_detector.py` → `fit_answer_levels.py` → `validate_detectors_mit.py` (~5 min) → `build_answer_evidence.py` → `make_listening_list.py` → `compare_settings.py` (Stage 10, ~1 min). Stage 11 (Phase 3): `run_style_tagger.py` (~29 min GPU) → `analyze_style_tagger.py` → `run_confidence_partb.py` → `extract_partb_embeddings.py --model wavlm_large` / `--model audeering` (~28 / 14 min GPU) → `run_confidence_partb.py --features b3`. Optional cross-check: `run_ser_windows.py --model odyssey --dataset mit` (~11 min GPU). Stage 12 (Phase 4): `test_new_recording.py --test answers` (~10 min GPU) → `test_new_recording.py --test segment --attempt 2` (~10 min GPU); any interview: `analyze_interview.py <audio>`. Stage 13 (Phase 6): `export_for_fusion.py` (~1 min CPU, after `fit_answer_levels.py` and `build_answer_evidence.py`). Stage 14 (Phase 5): `interview_summary.py` (~1 min CPU, after `fit_answer_levels.py`; rewrites `interview_cuts.json`, which `analyze_interview.py` reads).
